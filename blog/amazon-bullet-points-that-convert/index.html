How to Write Amazon Bullet Points That Convert

By Prathna Jeswani

Most Amazon bullet points read like they were written by the warehouse team, not the sales team. They list what the product is instead of selling why it matters — and shoppers scroll past them without absorbing a single one.

The five bullet points under your title are prime real estate. They’re read on every device, indexed by Amazon’s search algorithm, and often the last thing a shopper reads before clicking Add to Cart or clicking away. Here’s how to write bullets that actually do the selling.

Lead With the Benefit, Not the Feature

A feature is what the product has. A benefit is what the customer gets. Most bullets stop at the feature and leave the customer to do the translation themselves — and most customers won’t bother.

Compare these two openings for the same insulated water bottle:

  • “DOUBLE-WALL VACUUM INSULATION” — a feature, dropped on the reader with no context
  • “STAYS ICE COLD FOR 24 HOURS: Double-wall vacuum insulation means your drink is still cold at the end of a full workday” — the benefit up front, the feature as proof

The second version answers the question every shopper is silently asking: what does this do for me? Every bullet should open with that answer, then back it up with the spec.

The lead-with-benefit formula

A reliable structure: OUTCOME IN CAPS, colon, then one sentence explaining the mechanism. The all-caps lead-in acts as a mini headline for skimmers, and the sentence after it rewards anyone who stops to read.

Front-Load the First Three Words

Amazon truncates bullet points on mobile, often after 15 to 20 words before a “read more” cutoff. On the app, the first few words are frequently all a shopper sees without tapping to expand.

This means the first three to five words of every bullet carry the most weight in the entire listing. They need to contain the core claim, not a throat-clearing lead-in like “This premium product features…” Cut straight to the outcome or the differentiator.

Weak: “This high-quality backpack is designed with a durable, water-resistant material that will keep your belongings safe.”

Strong: “WATERPROOF PROTECTION: Sealed zippers and a coated 900D fabric keep your laptop and books dry in a downpour.”

Answer One Objection Per Bullet

Five bullets, five separate jobs. Repeating the same selling point five different ways wastes slots that should be closing separate doubts in the shopper’s mind.

A high-converting bullet set typically covers:

  • The single biggest reason to buy (the primary differentiator)
  • A durability or quality proof point (materials, testing, certifications)
  • A usage or versatility point (who it's for, how it fits their life)
  • A risk-reversal point (warranty, guarantee, easy returns)
  • A trust or brand point (what makes this seller credible)

Before writing a single word, list the five most common hesitations a real shopper would have about this exact product. Assign one hesitation per bullet. If two bullets are answering the same doubt, one of them is a wasted slot.

Use Specifics Instead of Adjectives

“Premium,” “high-quality,” and “durable” are the three most overused words in Amazon copy, and they carry almost no persuasive weight anymore because every competitor uses them too. A specific number or fact does more selling than any adjective.

Weak: “Made from premium, durable materials that last a long time.”

Strong: “Tested through 10,000 open-close cycles without a hinge failure — built for daily use, not just the unboxing photo.”

If you don’t have a hard number, use a comparison instead: “twice the capacity of the leading competitor,” “fits phones up to 6.9 inches,” “holds up to 40 pounds of gear.” Concrete beats vague every time.

Keep Formatting Consistent Across All Five Bullets

Inconsistent formatting is a subtle signal of a rushed or low-effort listing, and shoppers pick up on it even if they can’t articulate why. If bullet one opens with an all-caps header and bullet three doesn’t, the listing reads as sloppy.

Pick one structure and apply it to every bullet:

  • Same capitalization style for the lead-in on every bullet
  • Similar sentence length — if bullet one is 12 words and bullet four is 40, tighten the long one
  • Consistent punctuation after the lead-in (a colon, always, or never)
  • No bullet that trails off with an ellipsis or an incomplete thought

Write for Search, Not Just for Persuasion

Bullet points are indexed by Amazon’s search algorithm, which means they influence what searches your listing surfaces for, not just how well it converts once someone lands on it. Every bullet is an opportunity to naturally include a secondary keyword the title didn’t have room for.

The mistake is doing this clumsily — stuffing a keyword in that breaks the sentence's flow. Done well, it's invisible: a bullet about a water bottle's insulation can naturally include “insulated travel mug” or “24 oz water bottle” without reading like it was written for a robot instead of a person. If a keyword can't fit naturally, it doesn't belong in that bullet.

Avoid the Compliance Traps

Amazon suppresses bullet points that break its content policy, and unlike image suppression, this can happen mid-review with no warning. The most common triggers:

  • Claims like “best,” “#1,” or “guaranteed” without a way to substantiate them
  • Pricing, promotional, or shipping information (that belongs in the offer, not the copy)
  • Medical or health claims that require FDA substantiation and haven't been cleared
  • Competitor names or comparisons that name the competitor directly
  • Contact information, URLs, or requests for reviews

A suppressed bullet section is worse than a mediocre one — it can pull the whole listing from search results while under review. When in doubt, cut the claim rather than risk it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many characters should an Amazon bullet point be?

Amazon's technical limit is around 200 to 500 characters per bullet depending on category, but the practical target is shorter. Aim for one clear idea per bullet, roughly 150 to 200 characters, so the core claim survives mobile truncation.

Should Amazon bullet points start with all caps?

It's a common and effective convention, not a requirement. An all-caps lead-in of two to four words creates a scannable mini-headline before the explanatory sentence. Consistency across all five bullets matters more than whether you use caps at all.

Can I mention price or shipping in Amazon bullet points?

No. Amazon's content policy prohibits pricing, promotional, and shipping information inside product bullet points. That information belongs in the offer listing, not the description copy, and including it risks suppression.

Should every bullet include a keyword?

Where it fits naturally, yes — bullet content is indexed by Amazon's search algorithm. But a keyword forced into a sentence that breaks its flow does more harm than good. Persuasion for the human reader comes first; the keyword should read as effortless.

How do I know if my bullet points need a rewrite?

If your bullets open with adjectives like “premium” or “high-quality” instead of a specific outcome, if two or more bullets answer the same objection, or if the formatting is inconsistent across the five, that listing has room to convert better.
