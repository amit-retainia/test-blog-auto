How to Write Amazon Product Titles That Rank and Convert

By Prathna Jeswani

Most sellers write their Amazon title once, at launch, and never touch it again. That’s a mistake — the title is the single highest-leverage piece of copy on the entire listing. It’s the first thing the algorithm indexes and the first thing a shopper reads before deciding whether to click. Get it wrong and you lose both the ranking and the sale before the buyer ever sees your images or bullets.

The title has two audiences, not one

A good title is written for Amazon’s search algorithm and for the human scrolling past it on a phone. Most sellers optimize for one and ignore the other. Keyword-stuff for the algorithm and you get a wall of text no one reads. Write only for humans and you rank for nothing outside your exact brand name.

Front-load the words that matter

Amazon’s mobile app truncates titles after roughly 80–90 characters. Everything after that point might as well not exist for a scrolling shopper. Put your brand, primary keyword, and the single most important attribute in that first line — not your fifth-most-important feature.

  • Brand name first, unless your brand has zero recognition and a generic keyword will out-convert it
  • Primary search term next — the phrase you’d want to rank for if you could only pick one
  • The one attribute that actually drives the click: size, count, material, or use case

Follow Amazon’s category-specific title rules

Character limits and required elements vary by category, and Amazon enforces them with suppressions, not warnings. Before finalizing a title, check the specific style guide for your category in Seller Central — Grocery, Apparel, and Electronics all have different rules for what must and must not appear.

What’s almost always required

  • Brand name
  • What the product is
  • Key distinguishing attribute (size, color, quantity, model)

What gets titles suppressed

  • ALL CAPS words used for emphasis
  • Promotional language: “best,” “free shipping,” “% off”
  • Symbols not part of the actual product name: !, $, ~, *
  • Seller name repeated redundantly with the brand name

Match title keywords to actual search behavior

Guessing keywords from intuition is how titles end up ranking for terms nobody searches. Pull your top search terms from Amazon Brand Analytics or a rank tracker, and confirm the exact phrasing shoppers use — “stainless steel water bottle” and “metal water bottle” can have very different search volume even though they mean the same thing to a human.

Write for the click, not just the crawl

A title can rank perfectly and still convert badly if it reads like a keyword dump. Once the required keywords and attributes are in, the remaining words should read like something a person would actually say out loud. A title a shopper can scan and immediately understand beats one that’s technically more keyword-dense.

Test before you commit to a rewrite

A title change affects ranking and historical review continuity, so don’t treat it casually. Use Amazon’s Manage Your Experiments (if eligible) to A/B test a new title against the current one on click-through and conversion rate before rolling it out permanently.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should an Amazon product title be?

Amazon allows up to 200 characters in most categories, but the first 80–90 characters are what actually get seen on mobile before truncation. Write those first words as if they’re the whole title.

Does keyword stuffing still work for Amazon SEO?

No. Amazon’s A9/A10 algorithm weighs relevance and conversion signals, and a stuffed title that hurts click-through rate will rank worse over time, not better.

Should I put the brand name first in every title?

Usually yes, since Amazon’s guidelines expect it and it builds brand recognition across a catalog. The exception is a new or unknown brand competing on a generic, high-volume search term, where leading with the keyword can win more of the click.

How often should I revisit an existing title?

Check it whenever conversion rate drops, a competitor re-ranks above you, or Amazon updates a category’s style guide — not on a fixed schedule. Titles that are working don’t need churn for its own sake.
